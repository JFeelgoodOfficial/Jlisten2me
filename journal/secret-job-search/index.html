When you're looking for a new job and haven't told anyone at home

By John Parsons · November 10, 2026 · 4 min read

You told your manager it was a dentist appointment.The interview was at eight, so you could be at your desk by ten looking like you'd hit traffic.You took the recruiter's call in the car in a parking garage, engine running for the air conditioning.At dinner, when your partner asked about your day, you said it was fine.

A quiet job search runs on small covers. Each one is harmless by itself. Added together, they make two lives, and you're keeping both going while doing your current job well enough that nobody looks twice.

Why it's staying quiet for now

Your boss is the obvious one. Tell them you're looking and you've more or less given notice, without an offer to land on. You might get passed over for the next project, or eased toward the door before you're ready to walk through it. So you watch for small signs instead. You wonder whether they saw that you updated your profile. You wonder whether the last-minute "can you stay late Thursday" is a test.

Coworkers notice more than you'd like. The second doctor's appointment in a month, the nicer shirt on a Tuesday, the long lunch that ends with you coming back a little flushed. Even a work friend you trust can let something slip in a meeting without meaning to. Once one person knows, you're managing what they know too.

Your partner is the harder one, and the reasons are usually kind ones. Maybe you don't want to raise their hopes. If you mention the better salary or the shorter commute and then don't get it, they'll be disappointed along with you, and you'd rather carry that alone. Or maybe you can already hear the argument: the benefits, the stability, the timing, whether this is the right year to take a risk. You'd rather have that conversation with an offer in hand, when there's something real to talk about.

So the plan is to tell them once it's settled. Meanwhile, everything that happens in between goes nowhere. The interview that went great and the one where you blanked on an easy question. The final round that ended with a warm handshake and then a silence that keeps going. You come home from each of those and have to act like it was a normal day.

Friends would listen, but a lot of them know your partner, and the next time everyone's at the same table someone might ask how the search is going. Keeping a secret from your partner is hard enough without spreading it around.

Letting the cover story drop for an hour

Holding all of this alone makes it louder than it needs to be. Each rejection rattles around with nowhere to land. Each good sign builds into a fantasy you can't check against anyone.

Saying it to another person puts it back in proportion. You hear yourself describe the job you're chasing, and you can tell from your own voice whether you want it or just want out. You hear how you talk about the job you have, which might be harsher than you realized, or softer. Sometimes you notice that the hardest part of the whole thing is the hiding, and that the search itself is going better than it felt.

It also helps to have one place where the cover story can come off. For an hour you don't have to remember which excuse you used with whom.

How a call fits around a workday

You can call from anywhere, which works well from a parked car on a lunch break. If you're in Austin, we can meet in person on one of a few Wednesdays a month at noon, at a public spot we settle on after you book. A first name is enough. Stripe handles the payment, so I never see your card.

When we start, you tell me whether you want to be talked up before the next interview or want to let it out about the last one. I won't give advice unless you ask for it out loud. Silence is fine if you need a second to think.

I don't take notes and nothing is recorded. I don't know your boss, your coworkers, or your partner, and nothing you tell me gets repeated or used as an example. When the call ends, the secret is still yours to tell when you're ready.

What a career coach is for

An hour of being heard won't get you the job, and I won't rewrite your resume or coach you through salary talks. If you want help with the search itself, a career coach is the right person for that. If the strain of keeping it quiet has started following you into your sleep or your relationship, a licensed therapist can help you sort through it.
