When you promised your mom you'd never put her in a home

By John Parsons · November 3, 2026 · 5 min read

You said it years ago, probably at a kitchen table, and you meant every word.Never. You'd never put her in one of those places.Then came the falls, the burner left on, the nights she didn't know which house she was in.Now she lives in a room with her name on the door, and you drive home from visits feeling two things at once.One of them is relief, and that's the one you can't say.

The tours blurred together after a while. The lobby with the fish tank, the activity calendar by the elevator, the nurse who smiled at your mother and talked to you over her head. You signed the papers. You've heard her ask, sometimes more than once in a visit, when she can go home. Meanwhile the family house went on the market, and you spent a weekend sorting her recipe cards into boxes marked keep and donate.

Then a few weeks passed. You slept through the night. You went to the grocery store without checking your phone in every aisle. And each time you notice how much lighter you feel, the promise comes back around and sits on your chest.

The people you'd normally tell

Your mom is the one you'd have told everything to, once. You can't tell her this. When she asks to go home, you say soon, or you talk about the weather, and you walk out to the parking lot carrying the answer you didn't give.

Your brothers and sisters have opinions, and the ones who live farthest away tend to have the most. They call to ask whether you really looked at enough places, whether she's eating, whether that aide seemed rough with her. If you told them you were relieved, you'd be handing them proof of something they already half believe about you. So you report the facts and keep the rest.

Your spouse or partner lived through the hard years beside you. They drove her to appointments, gave up weekends, and heard about all of it every night for longer than either of you wants to count. They're glad it's settled, and they're tired of the subject. You can see it on their face when you bring it up again, so you've mostly stopped bringing it up.

Friends mean well. Most of them haven't done this yet. They tell you that you did the right thing, which is kind, and it closes the conversation right where you needed it to open. A few tell you they could never do that to their own mother, and you add it to the pile.

When relief and guilt share a sentence

Relief and guilt arrive in your head together and argue, and the argument never finishes because nobody hears the whole thing.

Saying it to another person puts it in some kind of order. You start telling it as a story about her, and somewhere along the way it becomes a story about you, about the years you were the one who got the calls. You describe the last bad night at the house and hear how close it came. You say the promise out loud, the actual words, and you notice who you were when you made it and what you knew back then.

You might get to the word relief halfway through the hour, or at the very end, or in the first minute because it's been waiting by the door. Whenever it comes out, nobody in the room flinches, and you get to keep talking. You can also say that you check on her care more than you need to, that you read the staff's faces at every visit, that part of you is waiting to be caught doing something wrong.

How the hour goes

You can call from anywhere for $60, or meet me in person in Austin at a public spot we agree on, on one of a few Wednesdays a month at noon. Book under a first name if you like. Stripe takes the payment, so I never see your card.

At the start you tell me whether you want to be talked up or want to let it out. Some days you'll want someone to point out how much you carried. Other days you'll want to say the ugly parts and have them heard. Either is fine. I won't give advice unless you ask for it out loud. If you go quiet for a while, that's fine too.

I don't take notes and nothing is recorded. I've never met your mother or your siblings, and I have no opinion about the place you chose. When we hang up, what you said goes with you and nowhere else.

Where an hour stops

An hour of listening won't tell you whether her care is good enough, and it won't settle anything about the house, the money, or the paperwork. If you're worried about how she's being looked after, or you're weighing a move to a different place, a geriatric care manager or the facility's social worker knows what to look for and what to ask. If the guilt keeps coming back week after week, a caregiver support group puts you in a room with people who made the same promise and had to set it down. For Medicaid, powers of attorney, and selling the house, you need an elder law attorney.
