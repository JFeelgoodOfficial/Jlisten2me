# When you know layoffs are coming and can't warn your team

You were told in a short meeting with the door closed.
Keeping it confidential was a condition of being told.
Since then you've sat through three one-on-ones, and in one of them someone asked you straight out whether their job was safe.
You said something careful and moved to the next agenda item.

Being a middle manager before a layoff puts you in an odd spot. You know more than your team and much less than the people who made the call. Maybe you know the date and not the names. Maybe you know some of the names and not the reasons. You're still planning next quarter's projects in meetings, assigning work you suspect will never ship, nodding through a roadmap that half the room won't be around to finish.

And you're watching your own face. In standups, in the hallway, on video calls where you can see yourself in the corner of the screen. You worry that you look different, that someone will catch it, that the person who asked you directly will remember your answer later and decide you lied.

Underneath all of it there's a quieter thing. If your own job is safe, you feel guilty about that. If you don't know, you're carrying your own fear alongside everyone else's and can't show either one.

## Why you can't talk to anyone about it

The people who most deserve to know are the ones you're specifically not allowed to tell. Your direct reports trust you, and that trust is what makes this hurt. Warning even one of them, quietly, would feel like the decent thing for about a day. Then it spreads, the process falls apart, and you've put your own job and possibly theirs in a worse place. So when they ask, you deflect, and every deflection costs you something.

Your peers would understand better than anyone, and some of them might be on the list. You don't know which ones. Talking to a peer manager means either telling them something they don't know about themselves, or finding out they know more than you. Neither conversation is safe to start.

Your own boss is the person who told you, and they're under the same pressure you are. Going back to them with your feelings about it can look like you're struggling to handle what you were trusted with. You're aware that how you handle this is being noticed.

Your spouse or partner is where you'd usually take something like this. Here it feels like a breach, because you were told in confidence and you agreed. It also brings the fear home: if layoffs are coming, they'll want to know whether you're on the list, and if you don't know, now the whole household is waiting on the same date you are. You may decide it's kinder to say nothing, and then you're keeping a secret at both ends of the day.

Friends at other companies are a little safer, but your industry is smaller than it looks, and the people affected deserve better than to have it get around before they're told.

## What saying it out loud does

A secret like this sits in your body. It's there when someone laughs in a meeting and when you're making plans you know are pretend. Keeping it in all day takes steady effort, and you don't notice how much until you put it down.

Telling a person outside the company, who can't tell anyone because they don't know anyone, lets you say the whole thing at once: what you know, what you don't, what you said in that one-on-one, how it felt to say it. You might hear yourself admit the guilt about your own job. You might hear how angry you are about how this was handled, or how much you dread the day itself. It's all real, and nobody at work has room for any of it.

## What it won't do

An hour of talking won't change what's coming or tell you what you're allowed to say. For questions about the process itself, timing, what employees will be offered, what you can share and when, your HR team is the right place to ask. If you're being asked to do something that feels wrong, like misleading people in a way that sits badly with you or handling it in a way you think breaks the rules, talk to an employment lawyer about where you stand.
