# When the last kid left and the house is too quiet

You still buy groceries for more people than live here.
You walk past their bedroom and the door is open, the bed made, nothing on the floor.
The house used to be loud at dinner, and now you can hear the refrigerator.
You knew this day was coming for years, and you thought you were ready.

In your head you were. You helped pack the car. You said all the right things, and you meant them, because you're proud of who they turned into. Your chest didn't get the message. The sadness comes in waves, at odd moments: a song in the car, a cereal nobody else eats, a text that takes a day to come back.

Then there's the quieter thing underneath. For a long time, the kids gave the two of you a shared calendar, a shared worry, something to talk about over dinner. With them gone, you've noticed how long dinner feels when it's just you two. Your partner seems to be doing fine, maybe even enjoying it, and you can't tell whether that makes you feel more alone or more worried.

## The people you'd usually lean on

The kids are the first people you'd want to call, and the last people you want to burden. They're starting their own lives. If they hear how much you miss them, they'll feel guilty for leaving, or for being happy about it. You want them to go and not look back too often, so you keep your texts light and save the rest.

Your partner is right there, and that's what makes it strange. They miss the kids too, in their own way. They may have filled the evenings already, with a project, a hobby, a plan for the trip you always talked about. When you try to explain how heavy it feels, they offer solutions or a pep talk, and you end up feeling like you're grieving wrong. And if the quiet has started to show you how little the two of you share besides the kids, that's a hard thing to say to the person it's about.

Friends tend to have one line ready. "You'll love the freedom." Some of them are already there and do love it, and they mean it as encouragement. It still lands like a door closing on the conversation. Friends who haven't reached this stage yet will listen politely and change the subject, because it's their future you're describing.

Your own parents or siblings might understand best, and they also might turn it into a story about when you left home. That can be sweet, and it can take the hour away from you.

So you carry it. You're fine at work, fine at dinner, fine on the phone with the kids. The sadness comes out when you're alone in the house, which is more often now.

## Hearing it in your own voice

Inside your head, this kind of grief feels a little embarrassing. Nobody died. Your kids are healthy and doing what you raised them to do. So you talk yourself out of feeling it, and it comes back anyway.

Said out loud to another person, it gets to be what it is. You hear yourself describe the last morning before they left, and you notice which detail you lingered on. You hear yourself say something about your marriage you hadn't put into words before, and it doesn't sound as frightening as it did at 3 a.m. You may laugh at yourself about the groceries. You may cry about the bedroom.

All the person listening has to do is stay with you for it, with no stake in your family and no hurry to steer you toward the bright side.

## When to see someone else

An hour of listening won't bring the house back to how it was, and it won't fix a marriage that's started to feel empty. If you and your partner are realizing you don't know how to be just the two of you, a couples counselor can work on that with both of you in the room. If the low feeling hangs on for weeks and won't lift, or you've stopped caring about things you used to love, please see a licensed therapist or your doctor.
