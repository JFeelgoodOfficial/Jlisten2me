# When your marriage has gone quiet in the bedroom and you can't talk about it

You can't remember exactly when it stopped, only that you've started counting the months.
Bedtime has a routine now: one of you stays up late, one of you goes early, and nobody reaches across.
You lie next to someone you love and feel lonelier than you did when you were single.
And there's nobody you can say that to.

The loneliness of it is hard to explain to anyone who hasn't lived it. You share a bed, a mortgage, a calendar, maybe kids, and the one kind of closeness that used to belong only to the two of you has gone quiet. Each time you reached and it didn't land, it stung a little more, so at some point you stopped reaching.

## Why it stays unsaid

Your friends talk about their marriages, and somehow it's always the opposite complaint. Someone jokes about a spouse who won't leave them alone, everyone laughs, and you laugh along and say nothing. Saying your side out loud in that circle would turn a light joke into an awkward pause, and you can already picture the looks.

There's a fear under that one too: that people would decide something is wrong with you. That you're unattractive, or cold, or that your marriage is broken in a way they'd now be watching for at every barbecue. Even close friends talk, and this is exactly the kind of detail that travels.

Family is out of the question. Nobody wants a parent or a sibling picturing this, and whatever they said back would be worse than silence.

Your spouse is the person it's actually about, and the hardest person to tell. Maybe you've tried, and it turned into a fight, or into a flat apology and nothing changing. Every attempt risks another round of feeling turned down, this time in words. So you've gotten careful. You say you're tired too and roll over. They may have no idea how much it hurts, because you've worked hard to keep that hidden.

So it sits with you, at the end of every day, in the one room of the house where you're supposed to feel closest to someone.

## What putting it into words does

Most of what you feel about this has never been spoken, so it lives as a tangle of hurt, anger, embarrassment, and guilt that all arrive together around eleven at night. Saying it to another person makes you pull those apart. You hear which one is loudest. You might find the rejection stings more than the absence itself. You might find you miss being wanted more than anything physical.

There's also something steadying about saying it plainly to a person who doesn't flinch, laugh, or start fixing. The thing you've been treating as shameful turns out to be sayable, in ordinary words, at an ordinary hour of the day. Having said it once, you know you can say it again, whether that's later to your spouse or to someone whose job it is to help.
