# When it's time to take your dad's car keys

You noticed the new dent on the passenger door before he mentioned it.
He hasn't mentioned it.
Maybe a neighbor said something about the mailbox, or you rode along to the store and held the door handle the whole way.
You know he shouldn't be driving anymore, and you know exactly what that car means to him.

He'll tell you he only takes short trips. The pharmacy, the barber, the diner where his friends meet in the mornings. He's been driving since before you were born, and he'll remind you of that. You've caught yourself thinking about things you're a little ashamed of: hiding the keys, telling him the car needs a part that's on back order, letting the registration lapse and hoping he doesn't notice. Every version ends with you as the bad guy in your own family.

The conversation itself might take ten minutes. You've been having it in your head for months.

## Why you can't talk it over first

Your dad is the person it's about, and he's the last one who should hear it half formed. Bring it up before you've worked out what you're going to say and he'll dig in, and every try after that gets harder. You probably get one good first conversation, and you know it.

Your brothers and sisters might back you. They might also take his side, especially the ones who don't see him drive. One call from a sibling who says he seemed fine at Thanksgiving, and your dad has an ally and a reason to stop listening to you. Running it past them first means risking that before you've said a word to him.

His friends would tell him. They'd mean well, and it would still reach him secondhand, as something people have been saying behind his back. That's the worst way for him to hear it.

Your partner has heard you worry about this for a while and keeps saying you should just do it. That's easy advice from someone whose father it isn't, and you both know it, so you've stopped bringing it up at dinner.

## Saying it before you say it to him

This is a conversation that goes better if you've heard yourself say it once. In your head, the words sound firm and kind. Out loud, the first try often comes out crooked, with too much apology at the front or a run of his mistakes that sounds like a charge sheet.

So you say it to someone who won't react. You try the opening line you've been planning and hear how it sounds in the air. You try it again a different way. You notice whether you reach first for the dent, the neighbor, or the doctor. You say what you'll offer him, the rides and the standing trip to the store, and you hear whether you mean it. You say his answers back to yourself, the ones you already know are coming, and hear what you'd say to each.

Somewhere in there your voice will probably catch. It tends to be the part about him getting older, the part where the man who taught you to drive is the one who has to stop. Better to meet that feeling in a practice run than for the first time across his kitchen table.

You can also just say how much you hate being the one who has to do this. That part needs saying too, and he's the one person who can't hear it.

## Where an hour stops

I can't tell you whether your dad is safe to drive, and an hour on the phone won't make him agree to stop. His doctor is the right person to assess that. A doctor can order a formal driving evaluation, and depending on where he lives, the licensing office can review whether he should keep his license. Bringing his doctor in also means the verdict doesn't rest on you alone. If you're worried he's in danger behind the wheel right now, that conversation with his doctor shouldn't wait.
