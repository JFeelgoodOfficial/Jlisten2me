# When you're holding everything together while your partner is struggling

Your partner hasn't been themselves for months.
Some days they don't get out of bed, and most days they're quiet in a way that fills the house.
They're under a doctor's care, and you're grateful for that.
Meanwhile you're handling the dishes, the school run, the bills, and the cover story, and you're tired in a way you're ashamed to admit.

You love them. You also feel resentment sometimes, usually late in the evening when the last chore is done and nobody has asked how you are. Then the guilt shows up right behind it, because they're the one who's struggling, and who are you to be worn out?

## Why there's nobody to tell

Anyone you both know is off limits, because this is your partner's story too. Telling a friend how hard home has been means telling them how your partner is doing, and your partner never agreed to that. You protect their privacy even when it costs you.

Family gets the edited version. You've told your parents your partner has been busy, or under the weather, and you've skipped gatherings with reasons that sound fine. Each excuse takes a little out of you, and you've started losing track of which version you told to whom.

Friends would want to help, and some of them would judge. Maybe your partner, for not pulling their weight. Maybe you, for staying, or for complaining. Either reaction is one more thing to carry home.

Your partner is the person you'd normally talk to about something this big, and right now that's the one door you can't use. They already feel like a burden. Telling them how tired you are would hand them more weight on a day they can barely lift their own.

Your in-laws, if they're in the picture, tend to get defensive. Their child is struggling, and almost anything you say can sound like blame.

So you cancel plans, keep the house running, handle the kids or the pets or both, and keep your own needs at the bottom of the pile, where they've been for a while now.

## What it's like to say it once

When none of this has been said out loud, it sits in you as one heavy lump: love, fear, fatigue, anger, and guilt, all pressed together. Saying it to someone with no connection to your partner lets you pull the pieces apart. You can say you're angry and still mean that you love them. You can say you miss your own life and hear that both things are true at the same time.

There's also relief in talking about yourself for an hour. Your partner's care has a doctor and a plan. Yours, so far, has had nothing at all. Saying it plainly to someone who won't repeat it and has no opinion about your partner lets you be heard without exposing anyone.

You don't have to be fair in that hour, or generous, or calm. You've been all three for months.

## What the hour is

You can talk by phone from anywhere, which may be easier if leaving the house is hard right now. In Austin, you can also meet in person on a Wednesday at noon, at a public spot we agree on after you book. A first name is enough, and Stripe handles the payment, so I never see your card.

At the start you tell me whether you want to be talked up or want to let it out. After that, you talk. I don't give advice unless you ask for it out loud, and silence is fine. You can spend the first ten minutes on logistics and the rest on how lonely the house has felt. You can swear, or cry, or say nothing for a while.

I don't take notes and nothing is recorded. I don't know your partner, your family, or your friends, and nothing you say is repeated or ever used as an example. Their privacy stays intact, and so does yours.

## Where the hour stops

An hour of listening won't help your partner get better, and it won't lighten the load waiting at home. Questions about their care belong with their doctor. If you want steady support for yourself over time, your own therapist is the right person, and caregiver support groups exist for people carrying exactly this kind of weight.
