# When you lost your job and haven't told your family yet

You still set the alarm for the same time.
You shower, get dressed, and leave the house the way you always have.
Then you drive to the library, or a coffee shop across town, and sit there until it's time to come home.
The job ended a while ago.
Nobody at home knows yet.

Plenty of decent people have ended up exactly here. It rarely starts as a plan. It starts with the day it happened, when you meant to tell them that night and couldn't find the moment. Then the next night passed too, and the one after that, and somewhere along the way the silence became its own thing.

## Why you haven't been able to say it

Your spouse or partner is the person you most need to tell, and the person it's hardest to tell. For a lot of people, bringing money home is tied up with who they are in the family. Losing the job can feel like losing that place, and saying it out loud makes it official. You wanted to walk in with the bad news and the fix at the same time: I lost the job, and I've already found the next one. So you've been waiting for the second half of that sentence.

Meanwhile the days have their own small dreads. Payday comes and you watch to see if anyone checks the account. A bill arrives and you move it to the bottom of the pile. Someone asks how work was and you say "long," which is true in its own way. Savings go down a little every week, and you do the math on how long the cover can last.

The longer it goes, the more there is to say. At first you'd only have had to explain a layoff. Now you'd have to explain the mornings, the library, the weeks of "fine." That second part can feel worse than the first, even though it grew out of embarrassment and fear, and very little of it was ever meant to deceive anyone.

Your parents can be harder to tell than you'd expect. Maybe they were proud of that job. Maybe they'd worry out loud, or remind you of something they said years ago. Either way, there's a good chance it would reach your spouse before you could get there yourself.

Friends ask how work is going because it's a normal thing to ask. Saying "I got let go" to people who've always known you as the one with things handled takes a kind of nerve that's hard to find right now. Pride is part of it, and pride is a very human thing to have.

So you sit at the table in the coffee shop, apply for jobs, refresh your email, and keep the whole thing inside.

## Hearing the story in order

You've probably said the words in your head a hundred times, in a hundred versions. Saying them to an actual person for the first time is different. Your voice might shake. You might laugh at the wrong moment. You might hear yourself say "I didn't know how to tell them" and realize that's the truest sentence you've said in weeks.

Getting it out once, to someone with no stake in your family, can make it feel more sayable. You hear the story in order: the day it happened, the first night you didn't say it, the routine that followed. Heard that way, it usually sounds like a person who got scared and kept going. That's worth hearing in your own voice.

You can also say the parts you're ashamed of without watching anyone's face change.

## An hour at your own pace

You can call from anywhere, including the car or a quiet corner of the library. In Austin, we can meet in person on one of a few Wednesdays a month at noon, at a public spot we agree on after you book. A first name is enough. Stripe handles the payment, so I never see your card.

When we start, you tell me whether you'd like to be talked up or want to let it out. I won't give advice unless you ask for it out loud, and I won't tell you when or how to tell your family. Silence is fine. Some people need a few minutes before the first sentence comes.

I don't take notes and nothing is recorded. Nothing you say gets repeated or used as an example. When the hour ends, what you do next is still entirely up to you.

## When you need more than a listener

An hour of listening won't cover a bill or find you work. For the money side, your state's unemployment office can tell you what you may be able to claim, and a financial advisor can help with the numbers. If this has turned into something you can't shake, the dread, the sleepless nights, the feeling of being stuck inside the secret, a licensed therapist is the right person to talk to.
