# When you're the executor and your siblings think you're cheating them

Your name is on the paperwork because your mom or dad trusted you with it.
Now you have a folder of receipts, a spreadsheet with too many tabs, and a sibling asking to see all of it.
You spend weekends clearing out the house, deciding what happens to the good dishes and the decades of tax returns in the garage.
Somewhere in all of this, you're supposed to be grieving.

Every decision gets a question. Why that realtor. Why that price. Why you kept the clock from the hallway. You answer, and you attach the receipt, and the next message asks something else. Maybe a sibling did years of the caregiving and feels the equal split in the will ignores it. Maybe that sibling was you. Either way, you followed the will as written, sent out the shares, and then the texts stopped. Some of your brothers and sisters haven't spoken to you since.

The odd thing is how the grief and the paperwork run together. You cry over a utility bill in your parent's name. You find their handwriting on an envelope while you're looking for a bank statement, and you lose the rest of the afternoon.

## The people on the other side of it

Your siblings would be the natural people to share this with. They lost the same parent. Right now, though, they're the other side, and anything you say to them can turn into evidence. Admit you're overwhelmed and it sounds like you've made mistakes. Say you're hurt and it sounds like you're trying to make them feel guilty. So you keep the messages short and factual and hope nobody reads tone into them.

Your spouse or partner has watched your family do this for months. They're fed up. They want you to stop answering texts at dinner, resign as executor, or tell your siblings exactly what you think of them. You understand why. It also means that every time you bring it up, you get their anger at your family on top of your own.

Friends hear the details and the details sound small. An argument over a lamp, a dispute about who gets the car. Said out loud to someone outside it, the whole thing can sound petty, and you can see them thinking it. You know it's about your parent and about who each of you was to them. Explaining that takes more than a lunch.

The estate attorney handles the law, and you need them. They also bill for every call, and they're there for the probate deadlines and the filings. Breaking down on the phone with them about your mom's reading glasses is an expensive way to be heard, and you've noticed you hold it together on those calls.

## Hearing it in one piece

Inside your head, this comes in pieces. A text from your sister. A form due at the courthouse. A sweater that still smells like your dad. The pieces rotate, and none of them gets finished.

Saying it out loud to someone strings the pieces together. You start with the latest accusation and end up talking about the last time you saw your parent. You hear which part actually hurts more: being suspected, or the fact that the one person who could settle it for everyone is gone. You hear what you'd want your siblings to know if they'd listen for five minutes. You might hear that you're angry at your parent too, for leaving it like this, and you get to say that without anyone defending them.

I've worked in finance and payroll, so if you want to talk through the spreadsheet itself, it won't lose me. I won't audit it, and I'm on nobody's side. You can just as easily spend the whole hour on the house.

## What it won't settle

An hour of listening won't tell you what you owe your siblings in accounting, how to handle a disputed asset, or whether you're meeting your duties as executor. A probate or estate attorney answers those. If the family is stuck and you'd like to get everyone talking again, a mediator can sit with all of you and keep the conversation fair.
