# When you're getting divorced and your friends are picking sides

The group chat went quiet around the time the paperwork started.
The weekend barbecue happened anyway, and you saw the photos afterward.
A couple you've known for years still texts your ex on birthdays and hasn't texted you at all.
On the weekends the kids are at the other house, the hours stretch out with nobody to fill them.

Divorce changes who you live with, and then, more slowly, it changes who calls. Some friends pull back because they don't know what to say and are afraid of saying the wrong thing. Some heard your ex's version first and settled on it before you'd said a word. And in a circle built around couples, you've become the odd number at the table.

## Why the people around you can't hold it

Mutual friends are the obvious ones to talk to, and the riskiest. Anything you say can reach your ex by the weekend, sometimes word for word, sometimes worse. Even the ones who mean well get asked, and most of them are bad at pretending they haven't heard anything.

The friends who stayed close tend to make every conversation about the divorce. You meet for a drink, and within five minutes it's the lawyer, the schedule, the house. You're grateful they ask. You'd also like one evening where you get to be a person instead of a case file.

Family has feelings about your ex too. Some relatives are angry on your behalf, and their anger becomes one more thing you have to manage. Others were fond of your ex and still are, and you can hear them working to stay neutral. Either way, you end up editing what you say before you say it.

Your lawyer is on your side, and your lawyer bills by the hour. Their job is the case. Spending a billable hour on the birthday dinner nobody invited you to makes no sense to either of you, so you hold that part back.

That leaves the loneliest part of the whole thing, the part about who's still in your life, with nowhere to go.

## Saying it to someone outside the circle

Losing friends in a divorce is a strange, slow kind of grief. It happens through silence: a missing invite, a slower reply, a conversation that stops when you walk in. Saying it out loud gives it edges. You can name the people one at a time, say what each of them meant to you, and hear how much of your ordinary week went with them.

It also matters to talk to someone who has heard nothing from your ex. There's no version of the story to correct and no impression to defend yourself against. You get to tell it from the beginning, in your own words, to someone with no side to pick and no reason to repeat it.

You can say the unflattering parts too: the hurt at being left out, the anger at a friend who should have known better, the relief about one or two who drifted. Those are hard to say to anyone who might be at the next party.

## Who's better for the rest

An hour of being listened to won't repair friendships, and it has nothing to do with how your divorce turns out. Your lawyer is the person for the case itself, and anything about custody, money, or what you should or shouldn't say publicly belongs with them.
