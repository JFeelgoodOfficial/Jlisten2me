# When your grown child stopped speaking to you

The last message is still on your phone.
You've read it more times than you'd admit, looking for the sentence that explains everything.
When someone at work or in the checkout line asks how the kids are, you have a short answer ready that ends the conversation.
The holidays are coming, and there's a place at the table you've stopped setting.

Nobody holds a service for this. Nobody brings food. Nobody marks a date on the calendar. There was a last call, or a last visit, or a message that said more than you were ready for. Then quiet.

Some days you're sure it's all your fault and you go over every year of their childhood looking for the moment. Other days you can't make sense of it at all. Some nights you start a letter in your head. You apologize, then explain, then take the explanation back, then start over. You haven't sent it. You aren't sure you will. If there are grandchildren, you think about them on birthdays, and you count how old they are now.

## Who can't hear this

Your friends are the first people you'd normally turn to, and this is the hardest thing to tell them. There's a shame attached to it that a divorce or a layoff doesn't carry. Saying "my child won't speak to me" feels like handing people a verdict on your parenting. And the friends who do know tend to push. Just reach out. Life's too short. Show up at the door. They want it fixed, and they mean it kindly, and each suggestion lands as one more thing you've apparently failed to try.

Your other children, if you have them, are caught in the middle already. Some of them may still be in touch with their brother or sister. Anything you say to them becomes a message, or a burden, or a reason to pick a side. You don't want to put them there, so you talk to them about everything else.

Your spouse or partner is in it with you, and that makes it harder to talk to them about it. They grieve on a different schedule. They might be angry when you're sad, or quiet when you need to talk. Sometimes they blame you, or themselves, or the child, and every conversation drifts toward who did what. You can't think out loud with someone who's carrying their own half of it.

## What it sounds like out loud

I don't know what happened between you and your child, and I won't guess. That's yours to tell, in whatever order it comes out, from whatever side you're standing on that day.

Saying a thing like this to another person changes how it sits. You hear yourself say "I miss them" and "I'm angry with them" inside a minute and both stay true. You hear which memories you go to first. You might read the letter out loud, the one you haven't sent, and notice which lines you rush through and which ones you slow down on.

You can say the things you'd never say to your friends, like how much you dread the holidays, or how you skip past certain photos on your phone. You can say you're afraid you deserve it. You can say you're sure you don't. Nobody in the room hands you a ruling either way.

## The hour

You can call from anywhere for $60, or meet in person in Austin for $100 at a public spot we agree on, on one of a few Wednesdays a month at noon. You can book under a first name. Stripe takes the payment, so I never see your card.

At the start you tell me whether you want to be talked up or want to let it out. I won't tell you whether to reach out, and I won't take sides. If you ask for my thoughts out loud, I'll give them, and otherwise I'll just listen. If you need to sit quiet for a while, that's fine.

I don't take notes and nothing is recorded. I don't know your family and I won't meet them. What you say about your child, and about yourself, goes nowhere after the hour.

## What an hour can't do

An hour of listening won't bring your child back or tell you what went wrong. If you want to understand what happened, prepare for a conversation that may or may not come, or work through it over time, a family therapist is the right person. Support groups for estranged parents put you with people who know exactly what a quiet holiday sounds like, and some of them have been at it for years.
