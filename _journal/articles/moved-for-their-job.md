# When you moved for your partner's job and you're lonely in a new city

Your partner leaves for work in the morning and comes home with stories about people you haven't met.
You spent the day emptying one more moving box, walking to the grocery store, and checking your phone more than you'd admit.
Back home, everyone keeps telling you how lucky you are.
You agreed to this, and you'd probably agree again, and some days you can barely stand it.

The move made sense on paper. It was a real opportunity, the kind you don't turn down, and you both said yes to it. What nobody put on paper was the rest of it. Your partner walked into a building full of coworkers, lunch invitations, and a calendar that filled itself. You walked into an apartment.

You also left a job, or a career, or at least a version of yourself that people recognized. You used to have an answer when someone asked what you do. Now the honest answer is "I'm figuring it out," and it gets harder to say each time. Your life has shrunk to a few rooms and a few errands, and the arguments at home start over small things, the dishes or the thermostat, and rarely stay small.

## Who you'd normally tell, and why it stalls

Your partner is the obvious person, and the hardest one. They're happy, or trying to be, and they're doing well. When you say you're lonely, it can land as "your success is costing me," even when you chose your words carefully. So you watch them light up about their day and you keep the worst of yours to yourself. Then it leaks out sideways, over something small, and you both end up defending positions neither of you wanted.

Your old friends would listen, and they miss you. They also mostly see the photos. New city, nice place, your partner's big role. When you try to say how isolated you feel, someone says "but you're so lucky," and they mean it kindly. After a couple of those, you start leading with the good parts and hanging up early.

Your family may have had opinions about the move in the first place. Telling them it's hard can feel like handing them an "I told you so" they haven't said yet.

The people you've started to meet here are too new. Maybe a neighbor, someone from a class, a parent at the park, a few folks from your partner's office. Austin fills up with newcomers, so you're far from the only one starting over, and that helps a little. Still, three coffees in is no time to tell someone you resent the reason you live here. You're still deciding whether you like them.

That leaves you holding a lot of it alone, in a place where you're already alone most of the day.

## What happens when it's finally said

Kept inside, loneliness and resentment braid together until you can't tell which one you're feeling. You replay the decision. You rehearse arguments you'll never have. You wonder whether you're being ungrateful, and then feel guilty for wondering.

Saying it to another person pulls those strands apart. You hear yourself say "I miss my work" and realize how much of this is about that. You hear yourself defend your partner without meaning to. You notice which sentence you can't get through without your voice changing. Sometimes a piece you'd barely noticed turns out to carry most of the weight.

For that to happen, the listener only has to stay with you, keep up, and have no side to protect. You can say the ungenerous version, the one where you're angry at someone you love, and nobody gets hurt by hearing it.

## Where this stops

An hour of listening won't rebuild your career or make the city feel like home. If you're trying to work out your next job, or how to explain a gap, a career coach is the right person for that. If the loneliness has turned heavy, with weeks of not wanting to get up or not caring about things you used to care about, please see a licensed therapist. If the fights at home keep circling, a couples counselor can help you both hear each other.
