# When you're building a company and your partner doesn't get it

It's late, and you're checking the dashboard in bed with the brightness turned down.
Your partner asks, again, when the company is going to start paying.
You don't have a date, so you say "soon" and close the laptop.
At the last family gathering you told everyone it was going great.
Some weeks that's even true.

Somewhere along the way, the company stopped being something you talk about at home. It started with the arguments. Every conversation about the startup turned into a conversation about risk, about the paycheck you gave up, about whether this is fair to the household, and each one drained the energy you needed for the next morning. So you started editing. You share the good weeks. You keep the bad ones to yourself, because your partner already worries and you'd rather not hand them more.

That makes sense one week at a time. Over months it does something you never meant to do. The less your partner hears, the less they understand, and the less they understand, the more the company looks like a thing that takes you away and gives nothing back. You feel alone with the work. They feel alone with you. Both of you are right about that.

## Why nobody close can hear the whole thing

Your partner is the obvious person to tell, and the hardest. They're already anxious, and they have good reasons: the savings, the plans you've pushed back, the life they thought they were signing up for. When you mention a rough week, they hear it as evidence. You meant to say Tuesday was hard. They heard that the whole thing might fail, and now you're defending the company when you wanted to be listened to.

Your team needs you steady. They took a chance on you, and some of them took less money to do it. If you walk in tired and doubtful, they'll feel it within a day, and a few will start wondering whether they should be somewhere else. So they get the version of you that believes, even on the mornings that takes effort.

Investors are owed honest numbers, and you give them those. They're also the people who can ask for more say, push for changes, or slow down on the next round. Telling them that home is strained, or that you're unsure how long the household can carry this, hands them information they'll weigh. That's their job, and it makes them the wrong audience for this.

Family hears "it's going great" because anything else turns into a talk about getting a real job. Friends with salaries mean well, and plenty of them quietly think you should have stopped a while ago. Other founders understand the work, and many are dealing with the same thing at their own kitchen tables. They also trade in confidence, and admitting the strain in that circle can feel like it costs you something.

So the part you can't say anywhere is the middle part. You care about the company. You care about your partner. The two keep pulling at you, and you've gotten very good at hiding which one is winning on a given night.

## What happens when it comes out in one piece

Right now you tell this story in edited versions. There's the investor version, the team version, the dinner table version, and the one you give your mother. Each one leaves something out, and after a while you lose track of what the whole story even sounds like.

Telling it once, start to finish, to a person who sits in none of those rooms changes how it sounds to you. You notice which parts you've been cutting, and for whom. You might hear yourself make your partner's case better than they've managed to. You might hear how long it's been since you brought home a good week, or how tired you are of performing in two places at once.

The person listening has no position on whether the company works or whether you should keep going. They don't need you to sound confident. That's rare in your life right now, and it's most of what makes the hour useful.

## What the hour looks like

You can talk by phone from anywhere, or meet in person in Austin on one of a few Wednesdays a month at noon, at a public spot we agree on after you book. A first name is enough. Stripe takes the payment, so I never see your card.

At the start you tell me whether you want to be talked up or want to let it out. Some founders need someone to remind them why they started. Others need to say every frustrated thing they've swallowed at home. Either is fine, and you can switch halfway. I won't offer advice unless you ask for it out loud. If you go quiet for a minute, that's fine too.

Nothing is recorded and I don't take notes. I won't repeat what you say, and it never gets used as an example anywhere. I've never met your partner or your investors, and I have no reason to take a side.

## What it won't do

An hour of talking won't repair things at home, and it won't tell you whether to keep building. If you and your partner want help talking about this with each other, a couples counselor is the right person, because both of you get a voice in that room. If the question is the household money itself, how long the savings last and what you can safely put at risk, sit down with a financial advisor. I'm neither of those, and I'll say so if it comes up.
