# When you have to tell the boss who mentored you that you're leaving

The offer letter is signed and sitting in your inbox.
You're excited about the new job, and you've barely let yourself feel it.
Between you and that start date is one conversation with the person who took a chance on you.
You've rehearsed it in the shower all week and still can't get past the first sentence.

This boss invested in you. They pulled you into meetings you weren't senior enough for. They put your name forward when you didn't know it was being considered. They told you, more than once, that they saw you going somewhere. Now you're going somewhere, and it's away from them.

The guilt is real, even though leaving is a normal part of a career and you know it. You worry they'll feel used. You worry they'll make a counteroffer and you'll have to say no to that too, or worse, that you'll be tempted. You worry about the look on their face. And under all of it, you'd like to keep this relationship, because it's one of the best you've had at work.

## Why it's hard to rehearse with anyone you know

Your coworkers are out. Your boss has to hear it first, from you, and anything you say in the office has a way of traveling. Even a friend on the team would be carrying a secret they didn't ask for, and they might have feelings about you leaving, too.

Your new employer is thrilled to have you. They want you excited about the start date, and they'd rather not hear that you're anxious about walking away from the old place. Mentioning your guilt to your new manager feels like a bad first impression.

Your spouse or partner is supportive, and they've already heard you agonize about this more than once. They'll say the right things. They also can't play your boss. They don't know how your boss talks, what they'll ask, or how they go quiet when they're disappointed. Practicing with them only gets you so far.

Friends outside work might tell you it's just business and you owe nobody anything. That may be true, and it misses why this one is hard.

So the conversation stays in your head, where it gets longer and more dramatic every time you run it.

## Getting the first sentence out loud

In your head, this conversation has a dozen versions. In some you over-explain. In some you apologize too much. In some you end up listing every frustration you've had over the years, which you never meant to bring up.

Saying it out loud to a real person shows you which version you actually want. You hear how long your opening runs before you get to the point. You hear yourself lead with gratitude and notice how naturally it comes, because it's true. You figure out whether you want to ask for a private moment in person rather than sending an email, and how to ask for it without setting off alarms.

For a lot of people, the first sentence is the hardest part. Once "I've accepted another position" is out of your mouth, the rest tends to follow. You can let them react, whatever that looks like, and keep your own part brief. Grievances can stay unsaid. Thanks can be said plainly.
