# When your spouse's parent moved in and your marriage is paying for it

It happened fast: a fall, a phone call, and a few weeks later there was a third adult living in your house.
Your kitchen comes with commentary now.
You can't go out to dinner alone with your spouse without hearing about it when you get back.
And when you try to talk about any of it, you're told to relax.

Nobody planned this part. Your spouse's parent needed somewhere to go, and your house was the answer. You agreed, maybe because there wasn't time to talk it through. Since then, the privacy you used to take for granted has disappeared, and the small daily remarks about how you cook, how you clean, and what you wear have started landing harder than they should.

## Why you can't say it at home or anywhere else

Your spouse is the person you'd normally vent to, and this is their parent. You've tried, and they either changed the subject or said you're overreacting. They're at work when most of it happens, so they don't see what you see. You can feel how badly they want to avoid picking a side, and you understand why, which makes it harder to keep pushing.

Your own family would take your side right away, and that creates its own trouble. Tell your sister about the comments and she'll have a nickname for your in-law by Sunday. At the next holiday, everyone's polite to their face and cool underneath, and you'll know you started it.

Friends would listen for a while. Then the conversation drifts toward assisted living, and you hear how you sound: like someone who wants an aging parent out of the house. You mean something gentler and more complicated than that, and it keeps coming out heartless anyway.

Meanwhile the assisted-living question is already hovering in your marriage, half raised and never finished, because every time it comes up someone ends up hurt.

So the frustration builds with nowhere to put it, in a house where you can't even close a door and have a private phone call.

## Saying it straight, all of it

When frustration has nowhere to go, it comes out sideways: a sharp answer to your spouse, a cabinet closed harder than you meant. Saying it to someone outside the family lets all of it out, including the petty parts, without anyone getting hurt.

Once you start, more than complaint tends to come out. There's the strangeness of watching a parent figure get older up close. There's the worry about how long this goes on. There's the loneliness of feeling like a guest in your own home, and the guilt for feeling that way about someone who needs help. Hearing those laid out in your own voice can make it easier to tell which ones belong in a conversation with your spouse and which ones just needed to be said once.

## How the hour goes

You can talk by phone from anywhere, which might mean the car in a parking lot if the house has no private corner left. In Austin, you can meet in person on a Wednesday at noon, at a public spot we agree on after booking. A first name is enough. Stripe takes the payment, so I never see your card.

You start by telling me whether you want to be talked up or want to let it out. I don't give advice unless you ask for it out loud, and silence is fine.

Nothing is recorded, I don't keep notes, and nothing you say is repeated or ever used as an example. I've never met your in-law, and I have no opinion about how anyone in your family should live.

## What it won't fix

An hour of listening won't settle where your in-law should live, and it won't put you and your spouse on the same page. A couples counselor can help the two of you have that conversation with someone keeping it fair. For the practical side, an elder care manager or geriatric care manager can lay out the options, from help at home to assisted living, and what each one would involve.
