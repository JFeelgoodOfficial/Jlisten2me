# When you retired and don't know who you are anymore

You still wake up at the old time.
You check your email out of habit, and nothing there needs you.
For years people called to ask what you thought, and now the phone mostly rings with appointment reminders.
Your partner has a routine of their own, and lately you seem to be standing in the middle of it.

Everyone said you'd be living the dream. There was a party, a card with a lot of signatures, a few speeches. The first weeks felt like a long vacation. Then the days started running together. You reorganized the garage. You reorganized it again. You found yourself short with your partner over how the dishwasher gets loaded, and later you couldn't say why it mattered.

Somewhere in there, your spouse mentioned they'd like the house to themselves a few mornings a week. You said of course. It stung anyway. The two of you are working out who gets the kitchen when, who runs which errands, whether lunch is a shared meal now or everyone for themselves. Those talks feel small, and each one leaves you a little less sure where you fit.

## Who you'd usually tell

Your spouse or partner is the person you'd talk to about almost anything. This one is hard to bring to them, because they're part of the friction. Tell them you feel lost and it can sound like a complaint about the life they've built around the house, or a request for them to fill your days. You can see them bracing when you start, and you can hardly blame them.

Your former colleagues were the people who knew what your work was. They've moved on, the way you would have. The lunch you planned got rescheduled and then forgotten. When you do catch up, they talk about projects you'll never see finished, and you go home feeling like a visitor at your own old desk.

Friends who are still working envy you, and they say so. Complaining to them sounds ungrateful, like grumbling about a lottery win. Friends who retired earlier tell you to find a hobby, travel, volunteer, and maybe you will. Right now you'd like to say how it feels before anyone hands you a plan.

## What it sounds like said out loud

Kept inside, this turns into a low irritability that spills onto whoever's closest. You snap at your partner over the thermostat and feel bad about it all evening. You can't put a name to what's wrong, so it comes out sideways.

Saying it to a person gives it a shape. You start with the dishwasher and end up describing the meeting where everyone turned to you for the answer. You hear which part you miss most. It might be the title. It might be being asked. It might be the plain structure of a calendar that told you where to be at nine. You might hear yourself say "I don't know who I am now," and notice that it's the first time the sentence has been out loud.

You can also say the ungrateful things. You can say you're bored, that you miss being important, that some mornings you'd trade the freedom for a parking spot with your name on it. Those thoughts are easier to look at once they're said than they are while they're circling.

For an hour, someone is on the other end of the line waiting to hear what you think. That alone can feel strange at first.
