# When you have to give the eulogy and it's complicated

Someone in the family asked you to speak, and you said yes before you'd thought it through.
Now there's a blank page, a service in a few days, and a parent you had a hard time loving some years.
Everyone will be looking at you while you talk about them.
You want to tell the truth, and you don't want to hurt anyone, and you aren't sure those fit in the same five minutes.

You keep hearing the old rule in your head: if you can't say something nice, don't say anything at all. You can say nice things. There were good days, and you remember them. There were also years you'd rather not describe, and pretending they didn't happen feels like a lie told from a podium.

So you write a paragraph and delete it. You list their achievements and it reads like a résumé of a stranger. Then you remember the way they hummed while they cooked, or the car they kept far too long, and you start to see a version you could stand behind. Small, ordinary details often hold more truth than a list of accomplishments, and they let you be honest without settling scores.

## Why it's hard to talk this through with family

The people you'd normally run this past are grieving the same person, and they may have known a different one.

Your siblings might want a clean, warm version. Maybe they had an easier relationship with your parent, or maybe they've decided that today is no time for anything else. Either way, if you start explaining what you're struggling with, it can turn into an argument about whose memories count.

Your surviving parent, aunts, uncles, and family friends will be in the front rows. They want to hear something comforting, and some of them would be shocked by the version you carry. You can't test your doubts on them without making the week harder for everybody.

Your spouse or partner has probably heard the whole story already, more than once. They'll listen again, because they love you, and you can see it wearing on them. They may also have strong feelings about your parent that pull the conversation somewhere else.

Friends who never met your parent can be supportive, and they don't have much to hold onto. They'll tell you whatever you write will be fine.

That leaves you writing and rewriting alone, late at night, with no one to read it to.

## Saying the unsaid version first

There's usually a version of this eulogy you'd never give. The one with the anger in it, the disappointments, the thing they said that you've carried for years. It sits underneath every draft and makes them all feel false.

Saying that version out loud, somewhere safe, can take some of the pressure off it. Once another person has heard it, it may push less hard on the version you'll actually read. The true, kind sentences can come more easily after that, once you've stopped fighting to keep the other ones in.

Then there's the delivery itself. Reading it aloud is different from reading it in your head. You'll find the line that catches in your throat. Many people practice it out loud enough times the night before that they've cried it out already, and then bring a printed copy and a glass of water to the podium. Hearing yourself get through it once, start to finish, makes the morning less of an unknown.

## What I can't do for you

An hour of listening won't settle how you feel about your parent, and it won't write the eulogy for you.
