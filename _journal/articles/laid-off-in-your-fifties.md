# When you got laid off in your fifties and it feels like nobody wants you

You gave one company the better part of your working life.
Then a short meeting appeared on your calendar, with someone from HR you'd never met, and it was over before your coffee went cold.
Now you're applying for jobs you could do in your sleep and hearing nothing back.
When you do hear back, the job goes to someone young enough to be your kid.

You know how to work. You've trained half the people who were in that building. Selling yourself to a stranger half your age, reading your name off a screen, is a different skill, and every quiet week makes it harder to believe you'll get the chance to use it.

## Why you're keeping it to yourself

Your friends still think of you as the one with the solid job. Some of them have asked how retirement planning is going, or joked that you'll be the last one standing at that company. Telling them you're out, and that the search is going badly, means watching them recalculate who you are. Pride is a big part of it, and pride at this stage of life has a lot of years behind it.

Your spouse knows about the layoff. What they may not know is how the search is really going. You've seen what happens behind their eyes when the subject turns to money: the retirement date moving, the savings that were supposed to last, the house. If you tell them how few calls you're getting, you're afraid they'll start to panic, and then you'll be managing their fear on top of your own. So you give them the hopeful version and keep the rest.

Former colleagues are awkward in a different way. Some of them survived the cut and still work there, and they don't quite know what to say to you. Others went out the same door you did and are dealing with their own version. Reaching out to network feels necessary and a little humiliating at the same time, like asking for a favor from people who used to ask favors of you.

Your adult kids are the hardest to tell. You've been the one who helps them, with the car, the deposit, the advice. Saying you're struggling flips something that's been in place their whole lives. You don't want them worrying about you, or worse, offering money.

And there's the part that's hardest to say to anyone, because it's so hard to prove. The interviewer who asked how you'd feel reporting to someone younger. The word "overqualified." The feedback about culture fit. You've started taking older jobs and graduation years off your resume, and every time you do it, it stings. You suspect your age is the reason, and you can't show it, and saying so out loud can sound bitter even to you.

## Saying it to someone who isn't keeping score

When you've carried a story around this long without telling it, it gets tangled. The layoff, the searching, the money, the years at that company, the feeling of being set aside. Saying it from beginning to end to someone with no stake in your life lets you hear the whole shape of it.

You might hear how much of your identity was built around that one employer, and how strange it is to describe yourself without it. You might hear anger you've been too polite to show. You might hear yourself talk about what you're good at with more confidence than you've managed in any interview lately. And you can say "I'm afraid nobody wants me" without anyone rushing to talk you out of it, unless you've asked them to.

There's no audience for this to perform in front of. That alone makes it easier to be honest.

## What the hour looks like

You can call from anywhere. In Austin, we can meet in person on one of a few Wednesdays a month at noon, at a public spot we agree on after you book. A first name is enough. Stripe handles the payment, so I never see your card.

When we start, you tell me whether you want to be talked up, which some people like before an interview, or want to let it out. I won't give advice unless you ask for it out loud. Silence is fine.

I don't take notes and nothing is recorded. Nothing you tell me gets repeated or used as an example. I don't know your old company, your industry contacts, or your family.

## What it won't do

An hour of listening won't find you a job, and it won't tell you whether what happened to you was legal. If you think age played a part in how you were let go or how you're being treated in interviews, talk to an employment lawyer. For the retirement math, how long savings will last and what the next few years look like, a financial planner is the right person.
