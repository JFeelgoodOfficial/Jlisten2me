# When you have to let go of someone who's also your friend

It's happening next week.
HR has the paperwork ready and has asked you not to discuss it with anyone.
You've rehearsed the first sentence in the shower, in the car, and lying awake.
On Saturday you'll probably see them at a birthday party, and you'll have to act like nothing is coming.

You've known this person a long time. Maybe you started together, or you hired them because you trusted them, or the friendship grew out of years of hard projects and late dinners. Somewhere along the line you became their manager, and now the job needs something from you that the friendship can't absorb. Their work has slipped, or the role has changed, or the decision came from above you. Whatever the reason, you're the one who has to sit across from them and say it.

The dread is the worst part, and it grows. Every day between knowing and doing feels longer than the last. You're nicer to them than usual and hate yourself a little for it. You catch them laughing with the team and think about how that room is going to feel afterward.

## Why there's nobody to tell

Your team is close to this person too. They eat lunch together. Some of them are friends outside work. If you hint at what's coming, it reaches your friend before you do, and the one thing you can still give them, hearing it from you directly and with some dignity, is gone.

Your friend is the person you'd normally talk to about something hard. You've probably told them about worse things over the years. This is the one thing you can't bring to them, and that absence sits between you every time they ask how your week is going.

Mutual friends are a minefield. They'll want to help, they'll have opinions, and they'll know your friend well enough that a single careless remark gives it away. Even after it's done, anything you say to them now could be remembered as you talking behind your friend's back.

Your spouse or partner knows this person socially. They've had them over for dinner, maybe know their partner and kids. Telling them asks them to keep a secret at the next barbecue, and it puts them in the middle of a friendship that's about to change for both of you. You may decide it's kinder to carry it alone until it's over.

HR is the one place you're allowed to talk about it, and they're focused on doing it correctly, which is their job. They want the documentation in order and the meeting short. How sick you feel about it has no place on their checklist.

## What saying it out loud does

The thing you're dreading has two parts that are tangled together. One is the meeting itself: the words, the room, their face. The other is the friendship, and the real chance that it won't survive this. In your head those run together into one big weight.

Saying it to somebody outside lets you pull them apart. You can talk about the meeting and practice the opening line out loud if you want to. You can also talk about what this person has meant to you, and what it'll be like if they never speak to you again, without anyone treating it as a business problem. You might hear yourself say something you hadn't let yourself think, about what you owe them, or about how unfair the timing is, or about how long you've seen this coming.

You'll still have to have the conversation. It helps to have said the hardest parts once first, to somebody with no reaction that matters.

## What it won't do

An hour of being listened to won't make the meeting go well, and it won't save the friendship. For questions about the process, what you can say, what they're entitled to, how to document it, go to your HR team, and if there's something legally unclear, to employment counsel. I can't advise on any of that. If this has stirred up more than you can carry, a licensed therapist is a good person to talk to.
