# When you got a diagnosis and haven't told anyone yet

You left the doctor's office with a folder and a follow-up date.
You drove to work, or home, and acted like it was a normal day.
You've started the sentence a dozen times in your head and never finished it.
Right now you're the only one who knows, and you haven't decided how long that lasts.

There's a strange privacy to this stretch. Nothing about you looks different. You still answer emails, still go to the meeting, still laugh at the same jokes. Keeping the routine feels like the one thing holding the day together, so you keep it. Meanwhile there's a whole second track running in your head: who needs to know, who should hear it first, and how much each of them gets.

Part of what makes it hard is that every person's reaction is a separate thing you'll have to handle. Some will be kind. Some will be confused and ask questions you can't answer yet. Some will wave it off, and that might sting more than you expect. You're bracing for all of it before you've said a word.

## Why each conversation feels so loaded

Your family is the obvious first call, and you already know how it goes. Someone panics. Someone starts researching. Someone cries, and within ten minutes you're the one doing the comforting. They love you, and that's exactly why it turns into their fear, their plans, their need to do something. You haven't even sorted out how you feel, and you'll be managing how they feel.

Your employer is a different kind of risk. You may need time off for appointments, or you may need nothing at all for now. You're not sure whether to go to your boss first or to HR, or what each of them will do with it. You worry about being quietly moved off a project, or being seen differently in a review. So you keep showing up and saying nothing, which works until it doesn't.

Your spouse or partner is sometimes the hardest one of all. They'll take it on completely. They'll worry at night and look at you differently over breakfast. If things between you are already strained, or if they're carrying a lot themselves, you might want a few more days before you hand them this.

Friends mean well, and some of them will tell other friends. Coworkers who are also friends are trickier still, since anything you say can travel.

So you keep it to yourself, and the sentence you'd use stays unsaid.

## Practicing the words

There's a reason people rehearse hard conversations. The first time you say something like this out loud, your voice does things you didn't plan. You stall on a word. You hear yourself soften it, or say too much, or make a joke to get past the hard part.

Saying it first to someone with no stake in it gives you a place to hear all that before it matters. You find out which word you can't get through. You find out whether you want to lead with the facts or with how you're doing. You try the version for your mother and the version for your manager and hear how different they are. You might discover you're angrier, or calmer, than you thought.

Once the words have been out loud once, they lose some of their weight in your mouth. That first time is the hard one, and you get to spend it somewhere it can't go wrong.

## What the hour is like

You book a phone hour from anywhere, or meet in person in Austin on a few Wednesdays a month at noon, at a public spot we agree on after you book. A first name is enough. Stripe takes the payment and I never see your card.

At the start you say whether you want to be talked up or want to let it out. You might want help steadying yourself before a call with family. You might just want to say the whole thing, scared parts included, to someone who won't flinch. If you'd like to practice the actual words, go ahead, as many times as you like. If you want my honest reaction, ask out loud and you'll get it. Otherwise I listen, and silence is fine.

Nothing is recorded, I don't keep notes, and nothing gets repeated or used as an example. I don't know your family, your boss, or your doctor.

## Where I'm the wrong person

I'm not a medical professional, and an hour with me won't answer any question about your health, your treatment, or what comes next. Those belong with your doctor and your care team. If you're unsure what you have to tell your employer, or what protections might apply to you at work, HR can explain your company's policies, and an employment lawyer can tell you where you stand. If the weight of this has started to keep you up night after night, a licensed therapist can help you carry it over time.
