# When you're not sure you can make payroll

It's two days before payday and you've checked the bank balance four times since breakfast.
You already know what it says.
Last time you covered the gap with your own savings, and the time before that with a credit card.
Out on the floor, your people are working like it's any other week, because as far as they know, it is.

Running a small business with employees means carrying a number nobody else sees. Payday comes on a schedule. Customers pay when they pay. In the space between those two facts you move money around, wait on a check, push a supplier, and do the math again at night when you can't sleep.

What makes it heavy is who's on the other end. Every person on payroll has rent, kids, a car payment, a plan for the weekend. You know some of their families. You hired them, and somewhere along the way you started feeling responsible for more than their hourly rate. So you keep paying them first, and you pay yourself last or not at all, and you tell yourself next month will be better.

Meanwhile other things fray. You're short with the people at home. You skip the doctor's appointment because it's a weekday and you can't leave. Friends stop inviting you to things because you always say you're slammed.

## Why the people around you can't hear it

Your employees are the first people affected, and the last people you can tell. Maybe you've told them part of it, that things are tight, that you're watching costs. You haven't told them you're not sure about the next run. If you did, the ones with the most options would start looking, and they'd be right to. Losing your best people in a tight month makes the next tight month worse.

Your spouse or partner probably knows money is a worry. They may not know that the savings account you both think of as a cushion has been quietly feeding payroll. Telling them means telling them that, and watching the worry move into their face and stay there. Your kids, your parents, your siblings all get a version that sounds fine, because you don't want them carrying it.

Other business owners in town seem like natural people to talk to, and some of them would understand better than anyone. They're also your competitors, or friends with your competitors, or people who know your suppliers and your landlord. In a local business community, "I heard they're struggling" travels quickly, and it changes how people treat you. Some of them would mean well. A few might see an opening.

Your banker and your accountant need the facts, and you'll give them the facts. They don't need to hear how it feels to lie awake doing the math about people you care about. That part has nowhere to go.

## What saying it out loud does

When you carry a number like this alone, it grows. It shows up at dinner, in the shower, at your kid's game. It turns into a running list of what could go wrong, each item worse than the last.

Saying it to another person shrinks it back to its actual size. You have to put it in sentences: what's coming in, what's going out, what you've already covered yourself, what scares you most. Once it's said, you can hear it. Some of it is as bad as you thought. Some of it was the 3 a.m. version, and you'll notice the difference when you hear it in daylight.

It also helps to say the part about the people. The guilt, the sense that their families depend on a balance you can't promise. That part rarely gets said, because there's nobody to say it to who isn't affected by it.
