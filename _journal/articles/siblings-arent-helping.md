# When you're caring for your parents and your siblings aren't helping

You're the one who knows the pharmacy hours.
You know which doctor runs late, which bill comes due mid-month, and what your dad will and won't eat lately.
Your siblings know what you post in the group text, when they read it.
And the resentment you've been swallowing has started coming up at odd times: in the car, in the shower, at your own kids.

You've spent vacation days in waiting rooms. You've left work early to sit on hold with an insurance company. You've helped with homework at the kitchen table while filling a pill organizer with the other hand. Then a sibling who lives a few states away calls with a thought about whether Mom should really be on that medication, and after you hang up you stand in the kitchen for a while doing nothing.

Maybe you're an only child, and there's nobody to resent. That carries its own weight. Every decision runs through one head, and there's nobody who remembers your parents the way you do to think it through with.

## Why it stays in your head

Your siblings are the obvious people to tell, and the riskiest. You've typed "I need more help" and deleted it more than once. You can already hear how it would go: one gets defensive, one gets hurt, someone brings up something from when you were teenagers, and the old family roles snap back into place as if no time had passed. The last time you asked for something concrete, the group text sat there, read and unanswered. Saying the whole thing could start a fight that outlasts your parents.

Your parent can't hear it either. They already feel like a burden. They apologize when you drive them places. Telling them you're stretched thin would hand more guilt to someone who's already losing enough.

Your spouse or partner has watched the evenings and weekends disappear. They're supportive, mostly, and underneath that they resent the hours, and you can feel it. When you complain about your siblings, you're also reminding them how much of your time goes to your parents, and the talk turns into a different argument.

Your kids are too young to carry it, or they're old enough to notice and you'd rather they didn't. You want them to remember their grandparents fondly. You want them to see you handling it.

Friends ask how your mom is doing. You give them the medical update. The part about your brother would sound petty out loud, so it stays out.

## What happens when it comes out in one piece

Resentment kept quiet piles up without any order. A canceled visit from last spring sits next to this morning's insurance call and a remark your sister made at a holiday dinner years ago. It all feels equally urgent because it's all been waiting.

When you say it to someone, it starts to sort itself. You hear which part is about your siblings and which part is about being tired. You might hear what you'd actually want from them, and it may be smaller and more specific than you expected: one appointment a month, one bill, a phone call to Dad on Sundays. Or you might hear that you've stopped wanting anything from them and would like to stop waiting.

You can also say the unfair things, the ones you'd never say to your siblings' faces and might not entirely mean. They need somewhere to go. Said to someone with no stake in your family, they land nowhere and hurt no one.

At the start you tell me whether you want to be talked up or want to let it out. You might just want an hour where nobody needs anything from you. I won't give advice unless you ask for it out loud. You can start anywhere, circle back, or sit quiet for a minute.

I don't take notes and nothing is recorded. I don't know your siblings, and I'm on nobody's side in your family. What you say about your brother stays with you.

## The limits of an hour

An hour won't divide the work among your siblings, and I won't call them for you. If you want everyone at one table with someone keeping it fair, a family mediator or a geriatric care manager can run that conversation and lay out who can realistically do what. A caregiver support group puts you with people who know the waiting rooms and the silent group texts from the inside, and some of them have already had the talk you're dreading.
