# When you lead a congregation or nonprofit and you're running on empty

You stood up in front of everyone and said the right things in the right order.
Then you sat in the car in the parking lot for a while before you could start the engine.
The budget is short, a major donor hasn't called back, and three people need you before Friday.
Everyone brings their heavy things to you.
You haven't found anywhere to put yours.

The role asks you to be the steady one in every room. You're the person at the hospital bed, the one who signs off on the grant report, the one who answers the text at nine at night because a family is coming apart. Being steady for that many people uses something up, and the job has no slot on the calendar for saying so.

## Why everyone close to the work is too close

The people you serve are the first ones you'd never tell. Mention to one member that you're tired of the work, and it becomes a conversation in somebody's kitchen by the weekend. Most of them would mean well when they repeated it. It would still change how they look at you from the front of the room.

Your board is the hardest. They're supposed to support you, and many of them try. They also review you, set your pay, and decide whether you stay. When the board itself is part of the strain, a chair who wants a say in everything, a faction pushing a different direction, a meeting that turns personal, you can't take that complaint to the same table it came from. And with fundraising always pressing, admitting you're running low can sound like you're telling them the next campaign is in trouble.

Your staff look to you for steadiness, and their jobs depend on the place holding together. If they see how worn you are, they'll worry about the organization, and some of them will start quietly worrying about their paychecks.

Your spouse probably knows more than anyone, and that's part of what makes it hard. In a lot of congregations and small nonprofits, your partner is inside the same community. They sit in the same rows or work the same events. They have friends there. Whatever you hand them, they carry into rooms full of the people it's about, and they have to keep a pleasant face too. Many leaders stop bringing the worst of it home so their partner can still enjoy the place.

Denominational leaders, regional staff, and peers in the field can look like the natural outlet. They're also the people who place you, recommend you, or hear about you later. Saying "I don't know if I can keep doing this" to someone with a say in your next role carries a risk you can feel in your stomach.

Underneath all of it sits the expectation that you're the one with the answers. You're supposed to have the faith, the vision, the plan. Admitting you're empty can feel like failing at the one thing the role is built on. You're always on, and nobody has ever told you where the off switch is.

## Saying it to someone outside the walls

There's a kind of tired that comes from saying the encouraging version all day. You get so practiced at it that you can lose track of the other version, the one you'd say if nothing depended on it.

Saying that version out loud to a person with no connection to your community does something you can notice. The first few sentences usually come out careful, from habit. Then the careful voice runs out, and you hear what's underneath. Sometimes it's anger at the board. Sometimes it's grief about a person you couldn't help. Sometimes it's a plain sentence like "I'm tired," said for once without a reassurance tacked on after it.

Nobody on the other end needs you to be strong. Nobody will see you at the next service or the next gala. That's an unfamiliar feeling for someone used to leading, and most people find they say more than they planned to.

## What the hour is like

You can call from anywhere, or meet in person in Austin on one of a few Wednesdays a month at noon, at a public spot we pick after you book. A first name is enough. Stripe takes the payment, so I never see your card.

At the start you tell me what you want: to be talked up, or to let it out. I won't give advice unless you ask for it out loud. If you want to sit quiet for a minute, that's fine. You can spend half the hour on the board and the other half on whether you still believe in the work.

I don't take notes. Nothing is recorded, and nothing you say gets repeated or used as an example. I'm not a member, a donor, or anyone's colleague. When the hour's over, you're the only one who walks out with it.

## Where an hour stops

An hour of listening is not counseling, and it won't stand in for it. If the tiredness has settled in for months, if you dread every day of the work, or if it's spilling into your sleep and your marriage, talk to a licensed therapist or a pastoral counselor from outside your own community, someone with no tie to your board or your denomination.
