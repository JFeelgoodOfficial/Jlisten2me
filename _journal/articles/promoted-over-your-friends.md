# When you got promoted over your friends and it's lonely now

The lunch invitation used to come to you without anyone thinking about it.
Now you find out about happy hour the next morning, from the photos.
You manage the people you used to sit with, and the other managers still treat you like the new one.
You're on the edge of two groups and inside neither.

Getting the promotion felt good for about a week. Then the job started. Overnight, the people you used to complain about work with became the people you assign work to, review, and sometimes have to correct. A few of them took it well. One or two have been testing you ever since, slow to answer, quick to remind you of how things used to be done, polite in a way that has an edge.

You've caught yourself going both directions. Some days you slip back into old habits and vent to a report about a decision from above, then realize halfway through that you can't talk like that anymore. Other days you overcorrect into someone stiff and formal, a version of a boss you'd have rolled your eyes at a year ago. Neither one feels like you.

## Why every direction feels risky

Venting down is the habit you're trying to break. Your reports used to be your friends, and some still feel like it. When you complain to them now, it lands differently. It tells them the decision they're about to carry out is one you don't believe in, and it chips at their confidence in you and in the place. It also hands the ones who resent your promotion something to repeat.

Venting up feels like admitting you weren't ready. Your boss chose you, and part of what they're watching is whether that was the right call. Bringing them your loneliness or your frustration with a former teammate can sound like you're struggling with the basics. So you bring them updates and keep the rest.

Venting sideways to the other managers seems like it should work. They've been through this. But you're new, you don't know yet who talks to whom, and you don't know who's watching how you handle your first rough patch. Until you've earned some trust with them, saying the honest thing there feels like a risk you can't measure.

Your work friends, the ones who weren't promoted and don't report to you, are the trickiest. They know everyone involved. Anything you say about a former teammate has a real chance of getting back to them. And the friendship itself has shifted, whether either of you admits it.

Friends outside work will listen, but they'll mostly tell you that you earned it and to stop worrying. That's kind. It also skips past how strange and lonely this is, and that's the part you'd like to say without someone rushing to cheer you out of it.

## What saying it out loud does

When nobody can hear the whole thing, it comes out in leaks. A little at the wrong lunch, a little in a sharp email, a lot in your head on the drive home. The leaks are what get you in trouble.

Saying all of it in one place, to a person with no role in your workplace, lets the pressure out somewhere safe. You get to say the petty parts and the sad parts and the parts you're proud of, in one go. You'll probably hear which old friendship you miss most. You might hear that the resistance from one teammate bothers you more than you've let on, or that you're doing better at this than it feels like from the inside.

None of it has to be useful afterward. Some of it just needs to be said once, out loud, to someone who isn't keeping score.

## What the hour looks like

By phone from anywhere, or in person in Austin on a few Wednesdays a month at noon, at a public spot we agree on once you've booked. A first name works. Stripe takes the payment, so I never see your card.

You start by telling me whether you want to be talked up or want to let it out. If you need to hear that you were promoted for a reason, I'll say it. If you need to complain about the happy hour you weren't invited to, go ahead. I won't give advice unless you ask for it out loud. Silence is fine.

Nothing is recorded, I take no notes, and nothing you say is repeated or used as an example. I'm not your boss, your peer, or your report. I have no stake in how your team sees you.

## What it won't do

An hour of being heard won't teach you how to manage former friends or fix the dynamic on your team. If you want to build those skills, a management coach or a mentor who has made the same jump is the right person for that. This hour is only for saying what it's like. If the loneliness has spread well beyond work and stays with you, a licensed therapist is worth reaching out to.
