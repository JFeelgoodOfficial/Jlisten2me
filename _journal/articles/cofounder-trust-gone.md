# When you and your cofounder have stopped trusting each other

You've rehearsed the sentence in the car more times than you'd admit: "We need to talk about roles."
You still haven't said it.
Yesterday the two of you disagreed in front of the team, then spent the afternoon acting as if you hadn't.
The founders' agreement is open in another tab, and nowhere in it does it say what happens now.

It didn't start all at once. Maybe it was effort first, the sense that you were carrying more of the weight and being thanked less for it. Maybe it was the vision, two people who once wanted the same company slowly describing different ones in pitch meetings. Maybe it was who gets the final word, and how often that question now hangs over decisions that used to be easy. Whatever the order, you're at the point where you check what your cofounder says against what they do, and you suspect they're doing the same to you.

The fighting takes up room the work used to fill. A product call becomes a proxy war. A hiring decision turns into a test of who's really in charge. You catch yourself thinking about a buyout, or about walking away, and then you think about what the company would look like if anyone found out you were thinking it.

## Why there's nobody safe to tell

This kind of trouble is dangerous because of who it touches. The news alone can do damage, before anything has been decided.

Your team watches the two of you more closely than you think. They saw the disagreement yesterday, and they saw the pretending afterward. If you confide in even one of them, you've made them choose a side, and people who've chosen a side start to behave like it. A split at the top is exactly what makes good employees look around.

Your investors bet on the pair of you. If they hear that the founding team is coming apart, some will want to get involved, and their involvement may not go the way you'd want. You're also aware that a fundraise or an acquisition means diligence, and you worry about what shows up when someone careful starts asking how decisions get made. Telling them early feels like volunteering for that.

Your cofounder is the person you most need to talk to and the one you can least afford to talk to carelessly. Once you say "I don't trust you" or "I've thought about leaving," it stays said. You can apologize for the tone later, and they'll still know. So you hold it until you're sure you mean it, and holding it changes how you sound in every meeting in the meantime.

Your spouse or partner is invested in a very direct way. They've watched you give years to this, and they may already have feelings about your cofounder that they've kept polite. Bring this home and you'll get loyalty, which is kind, and also a second person whose view of your cofounder has now hardened. That makes it harder for you to stay fair in your own head.

Friends in the startup world know your investors, or know someone who does. A story like this travels well.

## What saying it out loud does

In your head, this plays as a running argument with someone who isn't in the room. You win most of those rounds. They're also exhausting, and they never end, because the other side keeps getting rewritten.

Saying it to a person who doesn't know either of you breaks the loop. You have to explain the history, and in explaining it you hear which grievances are about now and which ones you've been carrying since the early days. You may hear yourself describe your cofounder more generously than you expected. You may hear the opposite, and realize you've been softening it even for yourself. Either way, you hear what you actually think, in your own voice, before it has to be said to anyone who matters.

## What it won't do

Talking it through won't settle who owns what or how a split would work. If your founders' agreement is thin on buyouts, vesting, or what happens when one of you leaves, that's a conversation for a business attorney, ideally before anything is said that can't be unsaid.
