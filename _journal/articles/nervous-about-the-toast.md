# When you're giving the wedding toast and you're already nervous

The wedding is weeks away and you already feel it in your stomach.
You've written the toast, rewritten it, and read it to the bathroom mirror more times than you'd admit.
There's one line near the end you can't get through without your voice cracking.
You love these two, and you'd like to do right by them in front of everyone they know.

Maybe you're the best man, or the maid of honor, or the parent of one of them. Maybe you haven't spoken in front of a crowd since a school report. Either way, the toast has taken up residence in your head. You catch yourself rehearsing it in the car. You've recorded yourself on your phone, listened back once, winced, and deleted it.

Part of the nerves is ordinary stage fright: a room full of faces, a microphone, a glass in your hand. Part of it is how much the moment matters. These people mean a lot to you, and you'll get one shot at saying so out loud.

## Who's off the list for practice runs

The couple would be the natural audience, and they're the last people you want to bother. They're juggling caterers, seating charts, and relatives. Asking them to sit through your draft adds one more thing to their week, and it spoils the surprise anyway.

Mutual friends will be in the audience. If they hear the toast now, the jokes won't land on the day, and you'll be watching their faces to see whether they're bored the second time around. Some of them are also giving speeches, and it can turn into a quiet competition you never wanted.

Your family is too close to it. They know the couple, they have opinions about the couple, and they'll suggest stories you deliberately left out. Your partner or roommate has likely heard it four times already and has started nodding before you reach the punchline.

So you practice alone. Alone is useful up to a point, and then you need a real person on the other end, someone who'll sit through it without a stake in how it goes.

## What happens when you say it to someone

Reading to a mirror and reading to a person are very different experiences. With a person listening, your pacing changes. You hear where you rush. You notice the joke that only works in your head and the sentence that's twice as long as it needs to be. A lot of people find their toast gets shorter after the first real run, and better for it.

The line that chokes you up tends to behave differently too. Saying it to another human a few times takes the surprise out of it. You learn where to breathe, and you might decide you'd like to let your voice crack a little on the day, since that's part of what the couple will remember.

You'll also hear the usual advice from friends, like having a drink or two for courage. If that idea is already worrying you, saying so out loud to someone with no opinion about your drinking can help you hear what you actually want to do. Some people pick three friendly faces in the room to talk to. You might try that while you practice, imagining the listener as one of them.

## How the hour goes

You book a phone hour from anywhere, or meet in person in Austin on a few Wednesdays a month at noon, at a public spot we agree on after you book. A first name is plenty. Stripe takes the payment and I never see your card.

At the start you say whether you want to be talked up or want to let it out. Talked up might mean reading the toast and hearing that you can absolutely do this. Letting it out might mean ten minutes on how terrifying microphones are before you read a word. You can run the toast once or ten times. If you want honest reactions, like whether a joke landed or a section dragged, ask for them out loud and you'll get them. If you'd rather I just listen, I will. Silence is fine.

Nothing is recorded, I don't keep notes, and nothing you say gets repeated. I don't know the couple, and I won't be at the reception.

## What this won't cover

An hour of listening won't turn you into a polished speaker. If you'd like real technique, like breath control, projection, or how to stand, a speaking coach or a local public speaking club can work with you on that over several sessions. If the nerves go well past wedding jitters and are making daily life hard, a licensed therapist is the right person to talk to.
